Pairwise category overlap

What is this?

For every pair of the categories currently shown in the legend above, this computes two numbers that both describe how separated the two are in F1/F2 space, but in different ways.

Pillai's trace comes from a two-group MANOVA: it splits the total spread of both categories combined into spread around each category's own center (within-group) and spread between the two centers, and reports what share of the total owes to the between-group part. Bounded between 0 (statistically indistinguishable by position — total overlap) and just under 1 (almost entirely separate regions). It's a stable measurement — it does not get artificially inflated just by adding more tokens — but it does saturate near its ceiling: two comparisons that are both very well separated can both read ~0.98, with no way to tell which is actually more separated.

Hellinger distance instead fits a 2D Gaussian to each category directly (its mean and covariance) and measures how much those two fitted shapes overlap geometrically, with no ceiling effect — it keeps distinguishing degree of separation past the point where Pillai has flattened out near 1. Bounded between 0 (identical) and 1 (no overlap at all), and — unlike Pillai's trace — a genuine mathematical distance (it satisfies the triangle inequality), which is what makes it safe to average across several comparisons into one number, as the section below does.

The two should generally agree on which pairs are more or less separated. Disagreement usually means the ceiling effect above, or two categories with similar centers but noticeably different spread/shape, which Hellinger is more sensitive to. Only pairs with at least 10 tokens on each side are shown; when the color scheme crosses vowel with speaker or group, only pairs sharing the same vowel are compared — comparing across different vowels (e.g. /i/ vs /a/) is trivially "very separated" and not an informative comparison.

Euclidean distance (in whichever formant units/normalization is currently selected above) is included for comparison — it's just the straight-line distance between the two categories' mean points, ignoring shape entirely. Click a column header to sort by it. Sorting by Euclidean ascending and scanning for rows where Pillai/Hellinger are nevertheless high is exactly how to find pairs whose means nearly coincide but whose actual token clouds don't — the clearest demonstration of why this panel doesn't just report Euclidean distance on its own.

Speaker system distance

What is this?

For every pair of currently selected speakers (independent of whatever color scheme is active), this averages the Hellinger distance above across every vowel both speakers have at least 10 tokens of — one overall number for how different their whole vowel systems are, rather than one vowel at a time. Sorted closest pairs first.

The summary line splits every pair into within (both speakers in the same dialect group) and between (different groups). If speakers cluster more tightly within their own group than across groups, the within-mean should sit noticeably below the between-mean — that's the direct, aggregate answer to whether dialect membership is doing anything beyond ordinary individual variation. If the two numbers are close, whatever differences show up vowel-by-vowel above aren't adding up to much at the level of the whole system.

One thing worth knowing: unlike a fixed batch script run once over a whole corpus, these numbers are computed from whichever speakers and vowels you currently have selected in the sidebar — narrow the selection, and the comparison narrows with it. That's usually what you want while exploring, but it does mean these aren't a fixed, corpus-wide constant.

Dispersion (variance / SD)

What is this?

Variance and standard deviation describe how spread out a category's tokens are around its own mean — the same quantities the confidence ellipses are built from, and the same "shape" information that feeds the shape-term half of the Bhattacharyya/ Hellinger comparisons above. A tight, low-variance category is a precisely, consistently produced vowel; a high-variance one is produced more variably — more casual/careless speech, a merger in progress, or (in clinical contexts like the Rangel Navia et al. paper referenced elsewhere in this tool) a marker some studies use diagnostically.

SD is just sqrt(variance) — shown in the same units as the formant itself (Hz, z-score, etc., depending on your normalization choice), which is usually easier to read than variance (which is in squared units and can look artificially large). F1 and F2 are reported separately since a category can be tight in one and loose in the other — that's exactly what an elongated confidence ellipse is showing you visually.

Context contrast (A vs. rest / custom groups)

What is this?

Answers questions phrased the way phonetic-context claims usually are in the literature -- "F2 is lower after labials," "vowels are longer after voiced consonants" -- directly, instead of requiring you to read values off the Dispersion table above by hand for each context category separately.

Pick one or more environment categories (at whatever side/granularity is set in Environment context above -- Place or Voicing are usually the right granularity for place-of-articulation or voicing claims) as Group A. By default, Group B is everything else currently active in the Environment context filter -- i.e. "A vs. the rest of what you're looking at," which is the comparison most claims like this are actually making. Switch to custom groups if you need to pool specific categories on the other side instead (e.g. Group A = labial, Group B = velar + dental + alveolar, leaving anything else out of the comparison entirely).

The comparison runs separately per vowel (never pooled across vowels -- a vowel identity confound would otherwise swamp a genuine context effect, since /a/ and /i/ differ enormously regardless of context) and separately for F1 and F2 of whichever formant pair is currently selected above (raw Hz, Bark, Lobanov, Nearey, Labov -- works with any of them). If your file has both a start and an end time column, you can compare Duration instead.

Means/counts update live as you change the selection. The permutation significance test (shuffling the A/B labels and seeing how often a difference this large happens by chance) does not run automatically -- press Compute significance after you've settled on a comparison. This is the one part of this whole tool that isn't fully reactive on every interaction, on purpose: re-running a permutation test on every unrelated sidebar click (axis range, color scheme, an ellipse toggle) would add real lag for no reason. If you change anything about the comparison after computing, the p-value columns clear rather than silently showing a result for a different comparison than the one now configured.

N shown is token count; "speakers" is the number of distinct speakers contributing to that side -- worth a glance before trusting a token-level difference, since a handful of prolific speakers can dominate a token count without the pattern actually holding up across the sample.
